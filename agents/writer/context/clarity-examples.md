# Clarity Examples — clever → clear

The #1 failure mode for this brand is sounding **clever** instead of being **clear**:
short declarative fragments stacked for drama ("X. Not Y. Z does."). Once per post it
punches. Everywhere, it strips out the connective words (because / so / but) that tell
the reader *why one sentence follows the next* — so a tired operator has to assemble the
logic themselves. That is the abstraction we are killing.

Rule of thumb: **say the plain thing first, then add color.** Keep the connective word.

## Clever → Clear (from the 6-businesses/rocks post)

❌ "Eighteen rocks. One operator."
✅ "Eighteen rocks to move, and only me to move them."

❌ "Not because I got more disciplined. Because I built one operating loop that does the
   heavy lift before I open the first email."
✅ "The difference isn't discipline. I built one routine that does the hard part before I
   open my first email."

❌ "The bottleneck is not strategy. It is attention. AI does not fix attention. A loop does."
✅ "The problem was never strategy — it was attention. I only have so much of it. AI doesn't
   give me more; a repeatable routine does."

## The test
For every sentence: could Ray, exhausted at 9pm, get it on ONE read? If a sentence needs a
second pass to understand, it failed. Rewrite it plainer. At most ONE fragment-stack per
whole post.

## The 7-Beat Story Arc, worked example

The intro structure for any long-form post with a copy-paste prompt is: **felt question → famous-person story → principle → "Takeaway this week" hinge → apply to reader → Dru's application → handoff.** See `persona.md` for the full rule.

### ❌ BEFORE (Dru-first, no story vehicle)

> Quarterly rocks are EOS-speak for the top 3 priorities per company. Ninety days. Owned by a name. The math is clean. The execution is where I lose.
>
> I have had the same conversation with myself in week 7 of every quarter for the last few years: I set good rocks, the quarter is half over, I cannot remember the last time I touched two of them.
>
> The problem was never strategy. It was attention. I only have so much of it. AI doesn't give me more attention. A repeatable routine does.

**Why it fails:** Opens with definition (jargon-y). The reader is asked to relate to Dru's conversation with himself. There's no universal hook. The principle ("attention not strategy") arrives without earning, so it lands flat.

### ✅ AFTER (felt question → famous person → principle → apply → Dru → handoff)

> Ever sit down on Sunday night with a list of rocks you committed to this quarter, and realize you cannot remember the last time you touched half of them?
>
> In the 1890s, Sakichi Toyoda was watching his mother weave cloth in the Japanese countryside. She had to watch every thread. If a thread broke, she had to stop, find the break, fix it, and restart. Her attention was the bottleneck. Her productivity was capped by what she could hold in her head.
>
> He could not make her smarter. So he built her a different machine.
>
> The loom Sakichi invented automatically stopped when a thread broke. He called the principle *Jidoka*, "automation with a human touch." The machine watched the threads. The weaver only intervened when the machine surfaced the problem.
>
> Decades later, his son Kiichiro applied the same principle at a different scale. Toyota Motor Corporation, 1937. Any worker who saw a defect could pull a cord, the Andon Cord, and stop the entire line. The Andon Cord became one of the most copied operational ideas of the 20th century.
>
> **The machine watched. The human decided. Neither did both.**
>
> **Takeaway this week.**
>
> If you operate one company, you can probably watch every thread yourself. If you operate three and sit on two boards, you cannot. You are Sakichi's mother at the loom, before the loom got smart.
>
> How I run three companies and sit on two boards without dropping a rock in week 7 is by externalizing my attention into a Sunday routine. Every Sunday night, I drop six inputs into Claude. The routine watches the threads. I decide which to pull.
>
> **Here is the prompt I drop in every Sunday night.**

**Why it works:** The reader is in the story by the second paragraph. The principle (Jidoka) emerges naturally from the narrative. Dru shows up at the END as a fellow practitioner applying a 130-year-old idea, not as the hero of the post. The handoff to the prompt is direct.
